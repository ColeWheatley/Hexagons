01 · SAMPLING

The bestagon dividend

A square grid has two axes, and your visual cortex is a trained detector for both: every ridgeline that isn't perfectly horizontal or vertical shatters into a staircase. A hex lattice has three axes at 60°, staggered — sampling on it acts as a natural reconstruction filter. Same information budget, no staircase to see.

It's also a more honest neighborhood. A square has four neighbors that touch properly and four diagonal ones that lie about their distance. A hexagon has six neighbors, all equidistant, all sharing a real edge — which is exactly what you want when every edge carries its own slope and skirt data.

02 · PACKING

Seven of everything, forever

Hexagons famously don't subdivide into hexagons. But they aggregate: a hex plus its six neighbors form a flower, seven flowers form a super-flower, and so on up — the Gosper island hierarchy, the same construction behind Bill Gosper's flowsnake curve. In axial coordinates the whole scheme is one integer matrix, M(q,r) = (2q−r, q+3r) — multiplication by the Eisenstein integer (2+ω). Each application scales the lattice by exactly √7 and twists it +19.1066°.

PowFinder's streaming tile is a level-5 island: 7⁵ = 16,807 unit hexes, ~830 m across, ~0.6 km² of mountain. Gosper islands are rep-tiles — every level tiles the plane — so the same shape is the streaming unit, the LOD node, the eviction unit, and the thing on screen. The map literally is the data structure.

And the twist stays in. Straightening it out would buy nothing — it's baked into the instance matrices, it costs zero per frame, and each LOD ring wearing its own rotation looks rad. That's a spec, not an accident.

03 · SKIRTS

Every edge is a channel

A height per hex is not a terrain model. The mountain lives in the vertical faces between neighbors — where two caps meet at different heights, the gap needs a skirt. Give an edge two owners and the skirts z-fight; give it none and you see daylight through the mountain.

So ownership is structural: every hex owns exactly three of its six edges — SE, S, and SW. Its northern edges belong to its northern neighbors. Every interior edge in the grid ends up with exactly one owner, so the skirt layer covers each edge exactly once and renders instanced, like everything else.

Each owned edge is then sampled independently at bake time — the diamond between two hex centers is its own DEM query, stored as a signed decimeter delta plus an edge slope (a direction-agnostic gradient magnitude over that diamond's own footprint, not a shared scalar). That's what kills cliff aliasing: a center-only model averages a 90° cliff into a rolling hill, but per-edge deltas let the S face drop the full cliff height while SE stays dead flat, off the same shared vertex. These are the Δ and slope bytes you'll meet in the wire format below.

That Δ is its own relative height, separate from the parent-chain recon() offsets covered next: it's a signed decimeter difference measured directly between the two hex centers' own heights, so it stays exact however deep the tree gets — no chained rounding to inherit, no absolute fixed-point field anywhere in the record.

04 · WIRE FORMAT

The address is the array index

GSP3, the tile binary, stores the island as a heap: node (depth d, index i) has children 7i…7i+6, child 0 concentric. Write an index in base-7 and its digits are the walk down the tree — so no record anywhere carries a coordinate. Position is derived from one shared table, parity-tested byte-for-byte between the JS engine and the Python baker. And because children are consecutive, every subtree is one contiguous slice of the file — hold that thought; culling is about to cash it in.

Heights are offsets, not absolutes: recon(node) = recon(parent) + dH·0.1 m, with each dH baked against the parent's already-quantized value so error never accumulates — every node lands within ±5 cm of the DEM, at any depth. Children offset from their parent's mean are zero-mean and tiny, which is why the gzipped tile came out ~30% smaller than the old absolute-height format.

05 · CULLING

One test retires 343 hexes

Visibility is a separate hierarchical query, and the planner is deliberately dumb: it sees opaque node handles and bounding boxes — nothing about Gosper packing. A thin adapter supplies each node's seven children, its bounds, and its descendant range. The planner tests those against the camera's actual rectangular frustum — wide in landscape, tall in portrait — plus a conservatively expanded guard frustum with motion lead, so tiles are resident before you finish the pan.

The heap layout is what makes rejection cheap. Children sit at 7i…7i+6, so a subtree is a contiguous index range, and dropping it is a range op — not a traversal. The geometry query descends only to L3: rejecting a single L3 node discards its 7 L2, 49 L1, and 343 unit descendants without reading a byte of them. There is no per-unit frustum test anywhere in the engine — 57 tests, worst case, govern all 16,807 hexes of a tile.

06 · LEVEL OF DETAIL

One predicate, zero seams

LOD selection runs per instance, in the vertex shader. Every cap ships its parent's center as an attribute, and draws itself iff:

// aParentPos rides along as a per-instance attribute
draw ⟺ dist(camera, self)   > R(k−1)
     ∧ dist(camera, parent) ≤ R(k)

The parent evaluates the same distance for its own test, so the cut partitions the terrain exactly — no cracks, no double-draw, no CPU LOD work, no hand-tuned overlap bands. The bands are fixed world distances — 2 / 5 / 10 / 25 / 60 km, measured in 3D including altitude, which is why zooming out in 2D coarsens detail automatically. There is no quality slider. The 2D map is the 3D engine with uHeightFactor → 0.

While the camera moves, every island shows the same complete L3 mosaic — large, skirtless caps at every distance. On settle, the exact banded cut is built off-thread and swapped in atomically. Two states, one frontier, nothing in between.

07 · THE PAYOFF

Mountains, for free

The manifest carries every baked island's level-5 aggregate — mean height, bounds, slope, normal. At startup all of them render as one InstancedMesh: hypsometric tint, baked lambert, ~6 triangles per 830 m island, out to 60 km. Distant ranges are mountain-shaped before a single terrain tile is fetched, and resident tiles simply zero-scale their horizon instance.

This is the whole point of the hierarchy: querying every largest hex in Tirol simultaneously costs approximately nothing. The 1 → 7 → 49 aggregation you saw in the explorer above is the same query, run downward instead of up.
